# Introduction: Why the Economic Calendar Is Your Edge

Every trader and investor who has watched a position reverse violently on a quiet Friday morning has asked the same question: what just happened? The answer, more often than not, is sitting on the economic calendar in a red or orange folder. These scheduled data releases are not background noise. They are the periodic reports that tell governments, central banks, institutions, and traders where the economy actually is — and more importantly, where it is likely to go.

The economic calendar used by platforms like ForexFactory, TradingEconomics, and Bloomberg organises these events by impact. Red folder events are the highest impact releases — the ones that have historically moved markets by the most significant margin in the shortest time. Orange folder events carry high but slightly lower impact, still capable of triggering substantial intraday moves across equities, bonds, currencies, and commodities.

This guide covers every major red and orange folder event across the US, UK, and European macro calendar. For each release we explain exactly how the data is collected, what the different outcomes mean for the economy, and how markets typically respond. The final section then draws connections between these releases — showing how one data point feeds into another and how experienced analysts construct a coherent economic narrative from individual data points.

_**“The economic calendar is not a list of interruptions to your trading day. It is the heartbeat of the market.”**_

**PART ONE: INFLATION DATA**

# Inflation Data

Inflation is the single most important variable in modern macroeconomics. It drives monetary policy, shapes bond yields, affects currency strength, and determines the real return on every asset class. The following releases are the primary tools central banks and markets use to measure it.

## Consumer Price Index (CPI)

The CPI is the most watched inflation release in the world. In the US it is published monthly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), typically in the second week of the following month. The UK equivalent is produced by the Office for National Statistics (ONS) and covers CPIH (which includes owner-occupier housing costs) alongside the headline CPI figure.

### How It Is Collected

The BLS employs approximately 400 economists and statisticians who collect price data from roughly 75,000 businesses and 23,000 residential rental units each month. The process involves site visits, telephone surveys, and web scraping. Prices are collected for a “basket” of goods and services that represents typical consumer spending. This basket is divided into eight major categories: food, housing, apparel, transportation, medical care, recreation, education and communication, and other goods and services. The weights assigned to each category are updated every two years using the Consumer Expenditure Survey, which tracks the actual spending habits of approximately 14,000 US families.

In the UK, the ONS collects around 180,000 price quotes per month from approximately 140 locations across the country, covering around 720 different items.

### The Different Measures

Headline CPI includes every item in the basket including food and energy — the most volatile components. Core CPI strips out food and energy to reveal the underlying, more persistent inflation trend. The Fed has historically focused more heavily on Core CPI as a policy guide. Trimmed Mean and Median CPI, published by the Cleveland and Dallas Federal Reserve banks respectively, offer further refinements designed to strip out the statistical outliers in any given month.

CPI can be expressed as a month-on-month (MoM) change or year-on-year (YoY) change. The YoY figure typically commands more attention from markets. The PCE deflator (covered separately) is the Fed’s officially preferred measure, but CPI moves more markets moment-to-moment due to its earlier release and broader public awareness.

### What the Numbers Mean for Markets

A hotter than expected CPI reading (inflation higher than forecast) tells markets that price pressures are more persistent than anticipated. This typically leads to a repricing of the Fed funds futures curve — traders push out rate cut expectations or bring forward rate hike pricing. The immediate market response is usually: equities lower, bond yields higher (prices lower), and the US dollar stronger. Risk assets including gold can go either way depending on whether inflation is driven by demand (typically equity-negative) or supply shocks (more complex).

A cooler than expected reading has the opposite effect — rate cut pricing comes forward, bonds rally, equities often gain (particularly growth stocks which benefit most from lower discount rates), and the dollar softens.

## Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) Price Index

The PCE deflator is the Federal Reserve’s officially preferred inflation measure. It is produced by the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) as part of the Personal Income and Outlays report, typically released on the last Friday of the month for the prior month. Despite being the Fed’s preferred measure, it receives somewhat less immediate market attention than CPI simply because it is released later and markets have already partially adjusted to the CPI print.

### How It Is Collected

Unlike CPI, which uses a fixed basket approach, PCE uses a chain-weighted methodology that automatically adjusts for consumer substitution behaviour. If steak becomes expensive and consumers switch to chicken, PCE captures that substitution and weights accordingly. CPI does not — it continues pricing steak at its original weight. This makes PCE structurally lower than CPI in most environments and considered a more accurate representation of real consumer behaviour.

PCE draws its data primarily from business surveys — specifically from GDP and national income account data — rather than directly from households. The BEA uses data from retail sales, service sector surveys, and government transfer payment records to construct the index.

### Core PCE

The most important sub-component is Core PCE (excluding food and energy), which Fed Chair Powell and other officials reference most frequently in speeches and press conferences. The Fed’s 2% inflation target is specifically stated in terms of Core PCE. When market participants discuss whether the Fed is on track with its mandate, they are almost always comparing Core PCE to that 2% benchmark.

## Producer Price Index (PPI)

The PPI measures inflation at the wholesale/producer level — i.e., what businesses pay for inputs before those costs are passed on to consumers. It is released approximately two weeks after the prior month’s end by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, typically one day before CPI for the same reporting period.

### How It Is Collected

The BLS surveys approximately 25,000 establishments each month, collecting price data on goods and services at the point of first commercial sale — when a manufacturer sells to a distributor, for example, not when the final consumer buys the product. The survey is voluntary but achieves a high response rate due to its long history and institutional relationships. PPI covers three stages of processing: crude goods (raw materials), intermediate goods (semi-processed inputs), and finished goods (ready for sale to businesses or consumers).

### Why PPI Matters

Because PPI measures costs upstream, it functions as a leading indicator for CPI. When input costs rise sharply at the producer level, businesses typically pass those costs downstream to consumers within a few months. A spike in PPI therefore often precedes a rise in CPI by one to three months, giving traders and analysts advance warning of consumer inflation pressure. Conversely, falling PPI can foreshadow easing CPI — a dynamic that was important during the 2022-2024 disinflation cycle.

The services component of PPI — particularly PPI for Trade Services — has become increasingly watched in the post-pandemic era as the Fed focused on services inflation as the most persistent component of the overall price level.

**PART TWO: LABOUR MARKET DATA**

# Labour Market Data

The labour market sits at the second half of the Federal Reserve’s dual mandate. The Fed is tasked not only with price stability but with maximum employment. Understanding how labour market data feeds into policy decisions is therefore essential for any macro trader or analyst.

## Non-Farm Payrolls (NFP)

The Non-Farm Payrolls report is widely considered the single most important scheduled economic data release on the global macro calendar. Published on the first Friday of every month by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, it reports the number of jobs added or lost in the previous month across all sectors except farming, private household employment, non-profit organisations, and active military. The report typically covers the reference week that includes the 12th of the prior month.

### How It Is Collected

The BLS conducts two simultaneous surveys each month. The Establishment Survey (also called the payroll survey) contacts approximately 122,000 businesses and government agencies representing 666,000 individual worksites — covering roughly one-third of all non-farm payroll employees. This is the survey that produces the headline jobs added number. The Household Survey is a separate survey of approximately 60,000 US households that produces the unemployment rate. These two surveys often diverge, which is why the unemployment rate and payrolls headline can sometimes tell slightly different stories.

The data is collected from payroll records during the reference week, then processed through a series of seasonal adjustments to remove predictable patterns (e.g., holiday hiring in November/December, teaching year patterns). Seasonal adjustment is a frequent source of revisions — the BLS revises the two prior months of data every release, and conducts an annual benchmark revision in February using comprehensive state unemployment insurance records.

### Reading the NFP Report

The headline jobs number is the most watched figure but rarely tells the complete story. Analysts simultaneously examine: the unemployment rate (headline U-3 and broader U-6 which includes discouraged workers and part-time-for-economic-reasons), labour force participation rate (the percentage of the working-age population either employed or actively seeking work), average hourly earnings (the wage growth component which feeds directly into inflation expectations), and average weekly hours worked (a leading indicator of future hiring).

_**“NFP is not just a jobs number. It is simultaneously a growth indicator, an inflation signal, a consumption outlook, and a Fed policy trigger — all in one 8:30am release.”**_

## JOLTS — Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey

JOLTS provides a granular, real-time picture of labour market supply and demand that the NFP report cannot. Where NFP tells you how many net new jobs were created, JOLTS tells you about the underlying flows: how many positions employers are trying to fill, how many people quit voluntarily, and how many were laid off. Published monthly by the BLS with a two-month lag, it became one of the most market-moving releases of the 2022-2024 rate cycle.

### How It Is Collected

The BLS surveys approximately 21,000 non-farm business establishments and government agencies each month. The survey asks employers to report the number of job openings on the last business day of the reference month, the number of hires made during the month, and the number of separations — broken down into quits, layoffs/discharges, and other separations. The response rate has historically been around 60-65%.

### The Quits Rate — The Most Important Sub-Component

Former Fed Chair Janet Yellen described the quits rate as her favourite labour market indicator. When workers are quitting in large numbers, it signals confidence — people only leave jobs voluntarily when they believe they can find something better. A high quits rate therefore indicates a hot labour market where workers have pricing power, which feeds wage growth and ultimately inflation. The quits rate peaked at historic highs in 2022 before declining sharply through 2023-24 as the jobs market cooled.

The ratio of job openings to unemployed workers (the V/U ratio) became a key metric during the post-pandemic period. At its peak in early 2022, there were approximately two job openings for every unemployed person — an unprecedented labour market tightness that concerned the Fed deeply.

## ADP National Employment Report

The ADP report is a private-sector estimate of US employment change, published two days before the official NFP report. It is produced by ADP, one of the largest payroll processing companies in the US, in partnership with the Stanford Digital Economy Lab. Despite its limitations as a predictor of NFP, it serves as a key sentiment-setter in the 48 hours before the official government data.

### How It Is Collected

ADP processes payroll data for approximately 25 million US workers — roughly one in five private sector employees. From this proprietary dataset, the Stanford Digital Economy Lab applies statistical modelling to produce an employment change estimate. Crucially, ADP changed its methodology in 2022, moving away from simple correlation with NFP and toward a regression model that also incorporates sector-level employment trends, wage dynamics, and other macro variables.

The correlation between ADP and NFP has historically been weaker than many traders assume. There are months where ADP significantly over- or under-estimates NFP, which is why sophisticated participants use it as directional context rather than an exact NFP predictor. ADP also adds wage data — median change in annual pay — that gives additional wage growth information ahead of the NFP average hourly earnings figure.

## Initial & Continuing Jobless Claims

Unemployment claims are published every Thursday morning at 8:30am ET by the Department of Labor, covering the prior week’s data. They are the highest-frequency labour market indicator available, providing a weekly real-time signal of labour market health. Initial claims measure the number of people filing for unemployment insurance for the first time. Continuing claims measure those who have already filed and are still receiving benefits.

### How It Is Collected

The data is drawn directly from state unemployment insurance (UI) systems. Every state administers its own UI programme, and the Department of Labor aggregates the weekly filings electronically. Because it captures actual benefit claims rather than survey responses, it is considered a highly reliable, timely measure. The main caveat is state-level processing backlogs, which can sometimes cause temporary distortions (a state catching up on a backlog inflates the week’s number, for example). Analysts typically use a four-week moving average to smooth these distortions.

### Interpreting the Numbers

Historically, initial claims below 250,000 per week are considered consistent with a healthy labour market. During the post-pandemic expansion of 2021-2023, claims routinely printed below 200,000 — an historically low level. When claims begin trending up consistently above 300,000, markets start pricing recession risk. Continuing claims are watched for signs of whether those who lose jobs are finding new ones quickly — a rising trend in continuing claims suggests the unemployed are struggling to re-enter the workforce, which is a more bearish signal.

**PART THREE: GROWTH & GDP DATA**

# Growth and GDP Data

## Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

GDP is the broadest single measure of economic output — the total monetary value of all goods and services produced within a country over a specified period. In the US, GDP data is published by the Bureau of Economic Analysis in three sequential releases: the Advance estimate (first release, approximately four weeks after quarter end), the Second Estimate (approximately eight weeks after quarter end), and the Third Estimate (approximately 12 weeks after quarter end, also called the Final estimate). The Advance release typically moves markets the most due to its early timing and information value, despite being subject to the most revision.

### How It Is Collected

The BEA constructs GDP from the expenditure approach: GDP = Consumer Spending (C) + Business Investment (I) + Government Spending (G) + Net Exports (NX). Consumer spending data is sourced from retail sales reports, service sector surveys, and Consumer Expenditure Surveys. Business investment data comes from the Census Bureau’s surveys of capital expenditure. Government spending is drawn from federal, state, and local budget records. Net exports data is sourced from trade statistics compiled by the Census Bureau. For the Advance estimate, many of the input data series are incomplete, so the BEA uses trend-based estimation for missing components — these are then revised in subsequent releases as actual data becomes available.

### Reading GDP Releases

Two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth is the popular (though not official) definition of a recession. The NBER (National Bureau of Economic Research) is the official US recession arbiter and uses a much broader set of indicators including income, employment, industrial production, and consumer spending — which is why some GDP contractions are not declared recessions and some recessions begin before official GDP contraction.

Analysts dissect GDP by component: strong consumer spending is generally bullish; weakness in residential investment often precedes broader downturns; an improvement in net exports can flatter GDP while masking domestic demand weakness. The GDP Price Index (implicit price deflator) embedded in the GDP release also provides a broad inflation signal.

## Retail Sales

Retail sales is one of the most important monthly economic releases because consumer spending accounts for approximately 70% of US GDP. Published mid-month by the Census Bureau for the prior month, it provides a timely, high-level reading of household spending activity across 13 retail categories.

### How It Is Collected

The Census Bureau conducts the Monthly Retail Trade Survey, contacting approximately 5,500 retail and food service firms with paid employees. Respondents report their monthly sales figures. The survey covers a representative sample of businesses across all retail categories, and results are weighted to estimate total US retail spending. A separate annual survey of approximately 22,000 firms provides benchmarks for the monthly estimates. The data is seasonally adjusted to remove holiday and weather-related patterns.

### Core Retail Sales vs Control Group

The headline retail sales figure includes auto sales, which are volatile and can significantly distort the picture. Core retail sales (ex-autos) is therefore watched more closely. The most important sub-component for GDP calculations is the Retail Sales Control Group, which excludes autos, gasoline, building materials, and food services — this figure feeds directly into the BEA’s calculation of consumer spending in the GDP report.

## ISM Manufacturing & Services PMI

The Institute for Supply Management (ISM) produces two of the most respected forward-looking economic surveys in the world. The Manufacturing PMI is released on the first business day of the month for the prior month. The Services PMI follows on the third business day. Together they provide a monthly snapshot of whether the manufacturing and services sectors are expanding or contracting — and critically, they are available weeks before any hard data for the same period.

### How It Is Collected

ISM sends monthly surveys to purchasing managers at companies across the US. These individuals sit at the intersection of supply chains and production decisions and therefore have real-time knowledge of business conditions. The Manufacturing survey covers approximately 400 purchasing managers at companies across 18 industries. The Services survey covers approximately 370 purchasing managers across 18 service industries.

Respondents simply indicate whether each indicator (new orders, production, employment, supplier deliveries, inventories, and others) is Better, The Same, or Worse than the prior month. The ISM converts these responses into a diffusion index: a reading above 50 indicates expansion (more firms reporting improvement than deterioration), below 50 indicates contraction, and 50 is neutral.

### The Most Important Sub-Components

Within each ISM report, the sub-components carry distinct signal value. New Orders is the most forward-looking — it represents future production demand. Prices Paid is an inflation signal — when purchasing managers are paying more for inputs, this feeds upstream into PPI and eventually CPI. Employment sub-indices provide advance colour on the jobs market ahead of NFP. Supplier Deliveries (longer = demand exceeding supply capacity = economically hot) is historically used as a capacity pressure indicator.

_**“ISM above 50 = expansion. ISM below 50 = contraction. Simple rule — but the sub-indices beneath the headline tell the real story.”**_

**PART FOUR: CENTRAL BANK & RATE DECISIONS**

# Central Bank Decisions and Communications

## FOMC Rate Decision and Press Conference

The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meets eight times per year to set the Federal Funds Rate — the overnight borrowing rate between banks that serves as the base from which all other interest rates in the US are priced. Each meeting produces a rate decision and a written statement. Four times per year the meeting is followed by a press conference with the Fed Chair, and a Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) is released, including the closely watched “dot plot” — a chart showing each FOMC member’s projected path for interest rates.

### How the Decision Is Made

The FOMC consists of twelve voting members: the seven members of the Board of Governors, the President of the New York Fed, and four of the remaining eleven regional Fed bank presidents on a rotating basis. In the weeks before each meeting, Fed staff compile the Beige Book (anecdotal economic conditions from each district), the Greenbook (economic forecasts), and the Bluesheet (financial market conditions). FOMC members submit their economic projections before each meeting, and the two-day meeting involves presentations, discussion, and a vote.

In the weeks around each meeting, Fed officials give speeches and make media appearances. This communication process — often called “Fed signalling” — means markets frequently have a high degree of confidence about the outcome before the meeting itself. The surprise element is therefore often in the tone of the statement, the dot plot projections, or the press conference Q&A rather than the decision itself.

### The Dot Plot — Most Watched Piece of Paper in Finance

The Summary of Economic Projections includes individual anonymous projections from all FOMC members for the Federal Funds Rate at end of current year, one year forward, two years forward, and in the long run. Markets compare the new dot plot to the previous one to gauge whether the committee has shifted more hawkish (higher dots = fewer expected cuts or more expected hikes) or more dovish (lower dots = more cuts expected). A single dot moving can meaningfully shift the median, and median shifts often trigger significant repricing in rates markets.

## Fed Chair Speeches and FOMC Minutes

Outside of scheduled FOMC meetings, the Federal Reserve Chair delivers numerous speeches throughout the year — most notably at the Jackson Hole Economic Symposium in late August, which has historically been used to signal major policy shifts. The FOMC Minutes are published three weeks after each meeting, providing detailed insight into the discussion that took place, including the range of views among members.

Markets have learned to parse Fed language with forensic precision. The addition or removal of a single word — “patient,” “data-dependent,” “restrictive,” “appropriate” — can move rates markets by multiple basis points. The shift from “higher for longer” to “balanced risks” in 2024 was a seminal communications moment that caused a major rally in both bonds and equities.

## Bank of England (BoE) and European Central Bank (ECB) Decisions

The Bank of England Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) meets eight times per year to set the Base Rate. The ECB Governing Council also meets eight times per year to set the Main Refinancing Operations rate (and related rates). Both are red folder events on the macro calendar and can significantly affect GBP, EUR, and European equity markets.

The BoE publishes its decision at noon UK time, accompanied every other meeting by a Monetary Policy Report (formerly the Inflation Report) which includes detailed economic forecasts. The ECB publishes its decision at 2:15pm CET, followed by a press conference from the ECB President at 2:45pm. Like the Fed, the real market-moving content is often in the language of the accompanying statement and the president’s answers during the press conference.

**PART FIVE: HOUSING MARKET DATA**

# Housing Market Data

Housing is often described as the most interest rate-sensitive sector of the economy. When rates rise, mortgage costs increase, affordability deteriorates, transaction volumes fall, and construction slows. Housing data therefore provides an important window into how monetary policy is transmitting through the economy in real time.

## Building Permits and Housing Starts

These two releases are published together mid-month by the Census Bureau and the Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD). Building permits are an authorisation granted before construction begins — they are a leading indicator since developers must obtain them before breaking ground. Housing starts measure the number of new residential construction projects begun in the reference month.

### How They Are Collected

Building permit data is collected from approximately 20,000 permit-issuing places (local governments, municipalities, and building departments) across the US. Areas not covered by permit systems have construction activity estimated using census-based models. Housing starts are measured via a survey of residential building permit holders, combined with site visits to confirm whether construction has actually commenced.

**Building Permits & Housing Starts — Quick Reference Card**

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Published by** | US Census Bureau / HUD |
| **Frequency** | Monthly — released mid-month for prior month |
| **Key figures** | Total permits \| Single-family permits \| Total starts \| Single-family starts |
| **Strong print** | Construction activity healthy — supports lumber, materials, homebuilder equities |
| **Weak print** | Sector slowdown — often reflects mortgage rate pressure \| May foreshadow GDP drag |
| **Key relationships** | Permits lead starts by ~1 month \| Both reflect mortgage rate environment \| Feed into GDP residential investment |
| **Official release** | [Census — New Residential Construction](https://www.census.gov/construction/nrc/index.html) |

## Existing Home Sales and New Home Sales

Existing Home Sales (published by the National Association of Realtors) and New Home Sales (published by the Census Bureau) measure transaction volume in the housing market. Existing home sales represent the largest component of total home sales (typically 85-90% of all transactions) and are released monthly around the 20th-25th of the following month. New home sales, while smaller in volume, are considered a leading indicator because they are counted when contracts are signed rather than when deals close.

During the 2022-2023 rate hiking cycle, existing home sales fell to their lowest levels in decades as the “lock-in effect” — homeowners refusing to sell and lose their 3% pandemic-era mortgages for current rates of 7%+ — dramatically reduced inventory and transaction volumes. This dynamic made housing data particularly important to follow as a gauge of monetary policy effectiveness.

| **New Home Sales** | Census Bureau \| Monthly \| Counted at contract signing — more leading \| ~10-15% of total |

**PART SIX: CONFIDENCE & SENTIMENT DATA**

# Consumer and Business Confidence Surveys

## University of Michigan Consumer Sentiment

The University of Michigan Consumer Sentiment Index is one of the most watched US consumer confidence measures, released twice monthly: a preliminary reading on the second Friday of the month and a final reading two weeks later. It is particularly sensitive to inflation expectations, making it uniquely important during periods of rising or falling price pressures.

### How It Is Collected

The University of Michigan’s Survey Research Center conducts approximately 500 telephone interviews per month with a nationally representative sample of US households. Respondents are asked about their current financial situation, their expectations for the next 12 months, and their expectations for the broader economy over the next five years. The 5-10 year inflation expectations sub-component is closely watched by the Federal Reserve as a gauge of whether long-run inflation expectations remain “anchored” — a critical input into whether the Fed needs to respond aggressively to near-term inflation spikes.

|---|---|
| **Published by** | University of Michigan Survey Research Centre |
| **Frequency** | Monthly — preliminary mid-month, final end of month |
| **Key figures** | Headline sentiment \| Current conditions \| Expectations \| 1-year inflation expectations \| 5-10 year inflation expectations |
| **Low sentiment** | Consumer pessimism — spending may slow \| Foreshadows softer retail sales \| May be bond-bullish |
| **Key importance** | 5-10 year inflation expectations watched closely by Fed as anchor signal — a rise here is hawkish catalyst |
| **Official release** | [U. Michigan — Surveys of Consumers](https://www.sca.isr.umich.edu/) |

## Conference Board Consumer Confidence

The Conference Board Consumer Confidence Index is released on the last Tuesday of each month. While similar in purpose to the University of Michigan survey, it has a larger sample (5,000 households vs 500) and places greater emphasis on labour market conditions — respondents are asked whether jobs are “plentiful,” “not so plentiful,” or “hard to get.” This Jobs Plentiful vs Jobs Hard to Get differential has historically been an excellent leading indicator of the unemployment rate.

**PART SEVEN: TRADE, MANUFACTURING & INDUSTRIAL DATA**

# Trade, Manufacturing and Industrial Data

## Trade Balance

The US Trade Balance measures the difference between exports and imports of goods and services over the prior month. It is released by the Census Bureau and BEA approximately five to six weeks after month end. A trade deficit (imports > exports) means more dollars are flowing out of the US than coming in, which has implications for the dollar, for GDP (net exports is a direct GDP component), and for the current account balance.

The trade balance can also be distorted by tariff-related front-loading — when businesses anticipate tariff increases, they rush to import before the deadline, inflating the trade deficit in the short term. This dynamic was highly relevant during the 2025 tariff escalation cycle, making trade data unusually market-moving.

**Trade Balance — Quick Reference Card**

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Published by** | US Census Bureau / BEA |
| **Frequency** | Monthly — released ~5-6 weeks after reference month |
| **Key figures** | Total trade balance \| Goods balance \| Services balance \| Bilateral deficits by country |
| **Wider deficit** | Larger drag on GDP net exports \| May weaken USD over time \| Can reflect strong domestic demand |
| **Narrower deficit** | Smaller GDP drag \| Potentially USD supportive \| May reflect weak domestic consumption |
| **Key relationships** | Feeds directly into GDP net exports component \| Bilateral deficits politically sensitive (tariff driver) |
| **Official release** | [Census/BEA — U.S. International Trade (FT-900)](https://www.census.gov/foreign-trade/Press-Release/current_press_release/index.html) |

## Industrial Production and Capacity Utilisation

Published monthly by the Federal Reserve Board, Industrial Production measures the output of manufacturing, mining, and utility sectors — the goods-producing side of the economy. Capacity Utilisation measures what percentage of productive capacity is currently being used. Together they provide insight into whether the physical economy is running hot or cold.

Capacity utilisation above approximately 80% historically signals inflationary pressure — factories are running near their limits, which can cause supplier bottlenecks, longer lead times, and rising input costs. Below 75%, the economy has significant slack, which is disinflationary. This relationship connects industrial production directly to the inflation data in Part One.

| **High utilisation (>80%)** | Inflationary pressure building \| Hawkish signal for Fed \| Positive for materials sector |
| **Low utilisation (<75%)** | Economic slack — disinflationary \| Suggests rate cuts more viable \| Negative for industrials |
| **Official release** | [Federal Reserve — G.17 Industrial Production](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g17/current/) |

## Durable Goods Orders

Durable Goods Orders, published monthly by the Census Bureau, measures new orders placed with US manufacturers for goods with an expected lifespan of three or more years — aircraft, machinery, vehicles, computers, and defence equipment. Because these orders are typically large and lumpy (a single Boeing order can move the headline number by several percentage points), the ex-transportation and Core Capital Goods Orders (non-defence, ex-aircraft) sub-components are far more important to markets than the headline.

### Core Capital Goods Orders — The Business Investment Barometer

Core Capital Goods Orders (NDCA) is sometimes called the “business investment barometer” because it represents corporate spending plans on productive capacity — machinery, IT equipment, industrial hardware. A rising trend signals businesses are expanding capacity and investing in future growth. A declining trend suggests corporate caution, often preceding weaker GDP investment components and potentially softer employment as firms delay expansion plans.

Eurostat publishes flash estimates for both Eurozone CPI and GDP ahead of the final confirmed readings. **PART NINE: HOW THE DATA FITS TOGETHER**

# How the Data Fits Together: Relationships and Cross-Indicator Analysis

Individual data releases have limited predictive power in isolation. The real analytical edge comes from understanding how these releases connect to each other — which indicators lead, which lag, and how the aggregate picture informs central bank behaviour and ultimately asset prices. This section explains the most important cross-indicator relationships.

## The Inflation Pipeline: From PPI to PCE

Inflation does not appear suddenly. It travels upstream to downstream through a predictable pipeline. Understanding this pipeline allows analysts to anticipate CPI and PCE movements rather than simply react to them.

> • ISM Prices Paid (manufacturing and services) captures what purchasing managers are paying for inputs. This is the earliest inflation warning signal — available each month within the first three business days.
> 
> • PPI (Producer Price Index) translates those input costs into a formal measure of wholesale/producer-level inflation. A sustained rise in ISM Prices Paid tends to feed into PPI within one to two months.
> 
> • CPI reflects what consumers eventually pay once those costs flow through the supply chain. PPI typically leads CPI by one to three months in direction.
> 
> • PCE (the Fed’s preferred measure) then reflects the chain-weighted consumer cost of goods and services — published after CPI but carrying the most direct policy weight.
> 
> • The FOMC rate decision sits at the end of the pipeline — a direct response to the accumulated inflation data above, calibrated against the labour market and growth picture.

_**“ISM Prices Paid → PPI → CPI → PCE → Fed Funds Rate. This is the inflation transmission pipeline. Know where you are in it and you can anticipate rather than react.”**_

## The Labour Market Cascade: From JOLTS to NFP to Wages to Inflation

The labour market data points do not exist in isolation — they form a cascade of leading, concurrent, and lagging indicators that together describe whether the jobs market is tightening or loosening, and what that means for wage inflation and consumer spending.

> • JOLTS Job Openings are the most forward-looking: they tell you how many positions employers want to fill. A high level signals future hiring demand.
> 
> • JOLTS Quits Rate confirms whether workers are confident enough to leave voluntarily — a tight labour market indicator and wage growth precursor.
> 
> • ADP Employment provides a private-sector weekly payroll estimate ~48 hours before NFP.
> 
> • Initial Jobless Claims (weekly) provide a real-time check on whether layoffs are accelerating between monthly reports.
> 
> • NFP (Non-Farm Payrolls) consolidates the headline: actual net job creation across the economy.
> 
> • Average Hourly Earnings (within NFP) translates job market tightness into wage pressure — the link from labour to inflation.
> 
> • University of Michigan and Conference Board Consumer Sentiment then reflect how employed and well-paid workers feel about spending — which feeds into Retail Sales.
> 
> • Retail Sales confirms whether consumer confidence translates into actual spending, which becomes the consumption component of GDP.

## The Housing Feedback Loop: Rates → Housing → Inflation → Rates

Housing is uniquely positioned in the macro cycle because it connects monetary policy (interest rates) directly back to inflation (through shelter costs in CPI) and to growth (through construction and consumer wealth effects). This creates a feedback loop that can either amplify or cushion central bank policy.

> • Rate decisions by the FOMC determine mortgage rates (30-year fixed rates broadly track the 10-year Treasury, which responds to Fed policy and expectations).
> 
> • Higher mortgage rates reduce affordability, which is reflected in weaker Existing Home Sales and softer New Home Sales.
> 
> • Weaker sales lead to reduced construction — Housing Starts and Building Permits decline, reducing residential investment in GDP.
> 
> • However, Shelter CPI (the cost of housing services) is the largest single component of CPI, representing approximately 35% of the index. It lags actual market rents by approximately 12-18 months due to how the BLS measures it (using renewal rents across all leases, not just new agreements).
> 
> • This lag means even when actual market rents are falling (as happened in 2023), Shelter CPI continues rising — which kept headline CPI elevated longer than many analysts expected and complicated the Fed’s cutting decision.
> 
> • Once Shelter CPI eventually rolls over, it provides a powerful disinflationary impulse that gives the Fed more room to cut, which then reduces mortgage rates and potentially restimulates housing demand — completing the loop.

_**“The 12-18 month lag in Shelter CPI is one of the most exploitable structural relationships in macro. Actual rents turn first — CPI catches up later.”**_

## The Growth Cycle: ISM PMI → Industrial Production → GDP

The hardest GDP data, published quarterly with significant lag, is the final destination of a journey that begins with soft survey data published in real time. Analysts who understand this progression can form early views on GDP trajectories well before the official numbers are confirmed.

> • ISM Manufacturing PMI (first business day of month): The New Orders sub-component is the purest forward-looking signal. Sustained readings above 55 typically precede accelerating manufacturing output within 1-3 months.
> 
> • ISM Services PMI (third business day): Services account for ~70% of the US economy — this index therefore carries enormous GDP weight. The Business Activity and New Orders sub-components are most predictive.
> 
> • Industrial Production (mid-month): The hard confirmation of manufacturing and mining output. Strong IP follows strong ISM with a short lag.
> 
> • Durable Goods Orders (late month): Core Capital Goods Orders confirm whether the investment component of GDP is building or contracting.
> 
> • Retail Sales (mid-month): The Control Group feeds directly into the BEA’s GDP PCE calculation — the single closest monthly approximation of the consumer spending component.
> 
> • GDP Flash/Advance (quarterly): The consolidation of all the above. By the time it is published, sophisticated analysts have already constructed a GDP “nowcast” from the component data above.

## Central Bank Reaction Functions: How Data Feeds Into Policy

The Federal Reserve does not trade at 8:30am on the day of each data release. But over weeks and months, each data print adjusts the collective model of where the economy is heading — and the FOMC’s reaction function is well-enough understood that markets continuously update their Fed policy expectations in near-real time.

The Taylor Rule — a formula relating the Fed Funds Rate to deviations of inflation from target and output from potential — provides a simple framework for understanding this reaction function. When inflation is above target and unemployment is below its long-run natural rate, the Taylor Rule prescribes a higher Federal Funds Rate. The data releases covered in this guide are essentially the inputs into that formula.

### The “Fed Whisperer” Framework

Experienced macro analysts watch four things simultaneously to predict Fed behaviour: the direction of Core PCE YoY (is it above 2%? falling? stable?), the labour market trend (is unemployment rising or falling from its cycle low?), financial conditions (are credit spreads, equity valuations, and mortgage rates transmitting tighter or looser policy?), and growth momentum (is GDP running above or below potential?). When all four suggest the same direction, the Fed’s next move becomes highly predictable. When they diverge — inflation sticky but unemployment rising, for example — you get the “dual mandate dilemma” scenarios that produce the most volatile FOMC meetings.

**CONCLUSION**

# Reading the Economy Like a Book

The economic calendar is not a collection of isolated data points. It is a continuous, overlapping narrative — each release a chapter that either confirms or challenges the one before it. The traders and analysts who genuinely understand these releases do not simply watch for whether a number beats or misses consensus. They ask: does this data point change the direction of any of the key trends? Does it validate or challenge the Fed’s current stance? Does it open or close the gap between where the economy is and where policy assumes it to be?

The most powerful moments in macro trading — the regime changes, the pivots, the unexpected trends — almost never come from a single data point. They come from the accumulation of multiple data points across different corners of the economy all telling the same story simultaneously. When ISM Services falls below 50 at the same time as Initial Claims are rising, JOLTS openings are declining, and Retail Sales are disappointing, that is not four separate events. It is one event: the labour market is turning. And when that happens alongside still-elevated CPI, you have the conditions for the hardest Fed decision — the dual mandate dilemma.

Understanding each data release individually, as this guide has covered, is the necessary foundation. But the real skill is synthesis — looking across the entire calendar, sequencing the releases, and building a coherent, updatable view of the economic cycle in near-real time. That is what separates the traders who move markets from those who react to them.

_**“Every red and orange folder event is a piece of evidence. The job of the macro analyst is to weigh the evidence — not chase the print.”**_
